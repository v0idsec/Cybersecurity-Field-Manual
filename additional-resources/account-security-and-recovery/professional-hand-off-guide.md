# Account Compromise – Professional Hand-off Guide

**A quick reference for helpdesk staff, IT teams, cyber security professionals and incident responders.**

This guide helps professionals direct an affected person to the appropriate self-help resource while identifying situations that need additional intervention.

It is not a replacement for incident response procedures, organisational policies or formal investigation.

## 2. Establish the level of impact

Ask what happened, without making assumptions.

Useful questions include:

- Can the person still access the account?
- Did they enter their password into a suspicious website?
- Did they share a verification code?
- Did they download or run a file?
- Did they install an application?
- Did they approve a login or QR code request?
- Has their phone number stopped working?
- Has money been lost?
- Was personal or identity information exposed?
- Is a work account or device involved?

Avoid asking the person to send passwords, authentication codes or recovery codes.

## 3. Prioritise urgent situations

Some situations require immediate action beyond sending a guide.

### Financial loss or exposed banking details

Direct the person to contact their bank immediately using an official contact method.

Do not delay this while attempting account recovery.

### Suspected SIM swap

Contact the mobile provider immediately and secure banking and email accounts.

See [SIM Swap & Phone Number Recovery](sim-swap-and-phone-number-recovery.md).

### Work account or device compromise

Follow the organisation's incident response process.

Escalate to the relevant IT or security team. Preserve relevant evidence and avoid unapproved remediation actions.

### Malware execution

If a suspicious file was executed, or remote access was granted, assess the affected device.

Follow organisational isolation, investigation and recovery procedures where applicable.

### Government or identity information exposure

Consider contacting the affected government service and IDCARE.

For myGov incidents, use the dedicated [myGov Account Security](mygov-account-security.md) guide.

## 4. Send the right resource

The objective is to provide a simple next step.

For example:

> Based on what you've described, this guide should help you secure your account and work through the recovery process:
>
> [Insert the relevant guide link]
>
> Please follow the steps in order. If you cannot regain access or notice anything suspicious, contact us again.

Where the incident involves financial loss, identity theft or an organisational account, include the appropriate escalation contact rather than relying on a self-help guide alone.

## 5. Preserve relevant information

Where appropriate and permitted by policy, record:

- Date and time reported.
- Affected service or account.
- Reported symptoms.
- Suspected initial event.
- Whether credentials or verification codes were shared.
- Whether files were downloaded or executed.
- Whether financial loss occurred.
- Actions already taken.
- Relevant provider or incident reference numbers.

Do not collect passwords, one-time codes or unnecessary sensitive personal information.

For organisational incidents, follow evidence handling and retention requirements.

## 6. Follow up

A guide being sent does not mean the incident is resolved.

Where appropriate, confirm:

- The person regained access.
- Recovery details are under their control.
- Unfamiliar sessions were removed.
- Multi-factor authentication is enabled.
- Other accounts using exposed credentials were secured.
- Financial or identity issues were escalated.
- The device was checked if malware was suspected.

For business incidents, follow the organisation's incident closure process.

## 8. Important professional considerations

- Do not guarantee that an account can be recovered.
- Do not promise that changing a password removes all attacker access.
- Do not ask users to disclose authentication codes.
- Do not direct users to unofficial recovery services.
- Do not dismiss incidents simply because a user only clicked a link.
- Do not assume every clicked link resulted in malware.
- Do not perform actions on personal accounts without clear authorisation.
- Do not delay bank contact where financial information may be exposed.
- Do not treat a self-help guide as a substitute for incident response.

## Objective

Make it easy for professionals to give people a safe, useful next step.

A well-written recovery guide can reduce confusion, improve response times and help people take action before an incident becomes worse.
