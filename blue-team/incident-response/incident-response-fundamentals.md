# Incident Response Fundamentals

## Overview

Incident response is the organised approach to managing suspected and confirmed cybersecurity incidents.

The objective is not simply to remove malicious activity. A complete response must understand what happened, determine its impact, limit further damage, restore operations and reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

## Events, alerts and incidents

These terms should not be treated as interchangeable.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Event | An observable occurrence in a system or environment |
| Alert | A notification generated by a security tool, rule or monitoring process |
| Suspicious activity | Activity that may indicate malicious behaviour but requires investigation |
| Security incident | An event or series of events that compromises, or threatens to compromise, confidentiality, integrity or availability |
| Major incident | An incident requiring elevated coordination due to its severity, scope or business impact |
| Data breach | An incident involving unauthorised access to, disclosure of, or loss of protected information; the precise definition depends on applicable law and policy |

An alert is not automatically an incident. Equally, an incident may exist without generating an alert.

## Common incident categories

- Malware infection
- Ransomware
- Business email compromise
- Credential theft
- Unauthorised account access
- Privilege escalation
- Data exfiltration
- Web application compromise
- Cloud account compromise
- Denial of service
- Insider threat
- Lost or stolen devices
- Unauthorised configuration changes
- Supply chain compromise
- Exploitation of exposed vulnerabilities

## Incident response objectives

A response team should aim to:

1. Protect people and critical business services.
2. Establish whether an incident has occurred.
3. Determine affected users, systems, data and services.
4. Identify the initial access method where possible.
5. Understand attacker activity and persistence.
6. Prevent further compromise.
7. Preserve relevant evidence.
8. Restore systems to a trusted state.
9. Meet applicable reporting and notification obligations.
10. Address weaknesses identified during the incident.

## Roles and responsibilities

The exact structure depends on organisational size and maturity.

| Role | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Incident Commander | Coordinates response, priorities, decisions and escalation |
| Security Analyst | Reviews alerts, investigates activity and develops findings |
| Incident Responder | Performs containment, eradication and recovery activities |
| IT Operations | Supports endpoint, server, network and service recovery |
| System Owner | Provides application context and approves business-impacting changes |
| Management | Provides authority, resources and business-level decisions |
| Legal / Privacy | Advises on legal exposure, evidence handling and notification requirements |
| Communications | Coordinates approved internal and external messaging |
| External Specialist | Provides forensic, legal, recovery or incident response expertise |

One person may perform multiple roles in a small organisation. Responsibilities and decision-making authority should still be explicit.

## Severity and prioritisation

Severity should consider more than technical alert scores.

Assess:

- Business criticality of affected systems.
- Sensitivity and volume of potentially affected information.
- Whether privileged accounts are involved.
- Whether attacker access is ongoing.
- Whether lateral movement or persistence is present.
- Number of affected users, endpoints and services.
- Operational disruption.
- Potential safety implications.
- Legal, regulatory and contractual exposure.
- Confidence in the available evidence.

Example severity model:

| Severity | Example characteristics | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Active widespread compromise, ransomware affecting critical services, confirmed major data exfiltration | Immediate escalation and coordinated response |
| High | Confirmed compromise of privileged accounts or important systems | Urgent investigation and containment |
| Medium | Confirmed compromise with limited scope or contained impact | Prioritised investigation and remediation |
| Low | Isolated suspicious activity with no confirmed compromise | Investigate, document and monitor |

Severity definitions and response time targets must be established by the organisation. Do not treat this example as a universal classification standard.

## Incident documentation

Maintain a central incident record containing:

- Unique incident identifier.
- Date and time detected.
- Date and time reported.
- Reporter and reporting channel.
- Incident category.
- Current severity.
- Incident owner and response team.
- Affected systems, accounts and services.
- Confirmed facts and outstanding questions.
- Investigation timeline.
- Evidence references.
- Actions and decisions.
- Communications and escalations.
- Current status.
- Recovery and closure criteria.

Use a consistent timezone in the incident record. Record source timezones where relevant.

## Decision-making

Significant response decisions should be recorded, including:

- What was decided.
- Who authorised the decision.
- When it was made.
- What information supported it.
- Expected benefits and potential risks.
- Whether the action was successful.
- Any follow-up required.

This is particularly important for disruptive actions such as disabling accounts, isolating servers, blocking network routes or shutting down services.
