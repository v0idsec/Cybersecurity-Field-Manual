# Spear Phishing and Pretexting

## Overview

Spear phishing and pretexting use contextual information to make an interaction appear relevant or trustworthy.

A professional assessment evaluates whether employees apply appropriate verification procedures when a request appears familiar, urgent or authoritative.

This guide focuses on controlled simulations and organisational process testing.

## 1. Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a targeted form of phishing that uses information relevant to a particular person, role or organisation.

Common themes include:
- Internal project updates.
- Document sharing.
- Recruitment activity.
- Supplier communication.
- Finance requests.
- Executive correspondence.
- IT support notifications.

The assessment should measure whether employees verify unexpected requests and report suspicious activity.

## 2. Pretexting

A pretext is the context or explanation used to establish credibility during an interaction.

Examples of assessment scenarios:
- A caller claims to be following up an approved service request.
- A visitor claims to be attending a scheduled meeting.
- A simulated supplier requests confirmation of account details.
- A person claims to need help locating an internal department.
- A simulated executive request requires an approval check.

Scenarios must remain within approved boundaries and must not cause real financial, operational or personal consequences.

## 3. Target Research

Research should be limited to information necessary to design the approved scenario.

Potential sources:
- Public company websites.
- Public job advertisements.
- Published organisational structure.
- Public contact details.
- Public social media posts.
- Public supplier information.
- Published business processes.

Avoid collecting:
- Personal home addresses.
- Personal phone numbers.
- Family information.
- Sensitive personal information.
- Private account details.
- Information unrelated to the assessment.

## 4. Scenario Development

For each scenario, define:

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Objective | Security control being evaluated |
| Target group | Approved employees or teams |
| Context | Business situation |
| Channel | Email, phone or other approved method |
| Expected behaviour | Verification or reporting action |
| Measurement | Observable event |
| Safety control | Limits on the interaction |
| Stop condition | When the test must end |

## 5. Business Email Compromise Simulations

Business email compromise scenarios can evaluate processes around:
- Supplier bank detail changes.
- Unusual payment requests.
- Executive approval.
- Payroll changes.
- Invoice discrepancies.
- Sensitive document requests.

The assessment should not initiate real payments or alter supplier details.

Measure whether the employee follows the organisation's existing independent verification process.

## 6. Authority and Urgency

Authority and urgency are common social engineering themes.

Examples:
- A request appears to come from senior management.
- A request claims to be time-sensitive.
- A request references an internal project.
- A request claims that normal procedures can be bypassed.

Assess whether employees:
- Verify the request through a separate channel.
- Follow approval requirements.
- Escalate unusual requests.
- Resist pressure to bypass controls.

## 7. Verification Controls

Review the organisation's procedures for:
- Identity verification.
- Payment changes.
- Password resets.
- Account recovery.
- Supplier onboarding.
- Sensitive data disclosure.
- Executive approvals.
- Help desk requests.

A strong procedure should not depend solely on the caller or sender providing information that may be publicly available.

## 8. Safe Execution

During the assessment:
- Use approved identities and channels.
- Do not access real accounts.
- Do not request actual passwords or MFA codes.
- Do not initiate transactions.
- Do not make real changes to payroll or supplier records.
- Do not impersonate emergency services.
- Do not contact unapproved external parties.
- Stop if a participant appears distressed or the scenario creates unintended operational impact.

## 9. Evidence

Record only what is necessary:
- Scenario identifier.
- Time of interaction.
- Relevant process followed.
- Whether verification occurred.
- Whether escalation occurred.
- Outcome.
- Relevant technical or procedural evidence.

Avoid recording unnecessary personal conversations.

## 10. Reporting

Report:
- Scenario and objective.
- Approved target group.
- Verification process tested.
- Observed outcome.
- Control weaknesses.
- Business impact.
- Recommendations.

Focus on the process failure rather than attributing fault to an individual.
