# Physical Security Assessment Methodology

A structured methodology for planning, conducting and reporting physical security assessments.

The objective is to determine whether physical security controls effectively protect an organisation's people, facilities, assets and information.

## 1. Assessment planning

Establish the assessment objectives and boundaries before conducting any testing.

### Define objectives

Typical objectives include:

- Evaluate perimeter security.
- Assess access-control effectiveness.
- Review visitor management.
- Evaluate restricted-area protections.
- Assess surveillance and alarm coverage.
- Review server room and infrastructure security.
- Identify weaknesses in physical security procedures.
- Evaluate the effectiveness of security monitoring and response.

### Establish scope

Document:

- Approved buildings and facilities.
- Specific floors, rooms and restricted areas.
- Physical assets included in the assessment.
- Access-control systems in scope.
- CCTV and alarm systems in scope.
- Approved testing dates and times.
- Permitted testing techniques.
- Prohibited activities.
- Emergency contacts.

### Rules of engagement

Obtain explicit written authorisation for activities such as:

- Testing access-control systems.
- Attempting entry into designated test areas.
- Testing visitor verification procedures.
- Conducting approved social engineering scenarios.
- Testing alarm notification workflows.
- Inspecting restricted infrastructure.

Define clear stop conditions.

Examples:

- Unexpected access to a non-scope area.
- Contact with an uninvolved person.
- Activation of an emergency or life-safety system.
- Discovery of a safety hazard.
- Disruption to business operations.
- Any request from the designated client contact to stop.

## 2. Reconnaissance

Collect information about the facility and its security arrangements.

Potential sources:

- Public mapping services.
- Satellite imagery.
- Public property information.
- Organisation websites.
- Publicly available floor plans.
- Building directories.
- Public photographs.
- Job advertisements describing facility operations.
- Publicly disclosed security and compliance documentation.

Record relevant observations without trespassing or interfering with operations.

## 3. Perimeter assessment

Evaluate the physical boundary protecting the facility.

Review:

- Fences and walls.
- Gates and vehicle barriers.
- External doors.
- Windows and glazing.
- Loading docks.
- Service entrances.
- Emergency exits.
- External lighting.
- Signage and restricted-area markings.
- Landscaping and visibility.
- Boundary maintenance.

Assess whether perimeter protections are appropriate for the facility's risk profile.

## 4. Access-control assessment

Review how access is granted, authenticated, monitored and revoked.

Consider:

- Employee access credentials.
- Contractor access.
- Visitor registration.
- Temporary credentials.
- Access permissions.
- Restricted-area authorisation.
- Lost or stolen credentials.
- Terminated employee access.
- Access logs.
- Periodic access reviews.

Assess whether access permissions follow least-privilege principles.

## 5. Locking and entry systems

Review the suitability and management of physical locking systems.

Consider:

- Mechanical locks.
- Electronic locks.
- Key management.
- Master-key arrangements.
- Electronic access readers.
- Door controllers.
- Door-position monitoring.
- Emergency release mechanisms.
- Maintenance and inspection records.

Do not force, manipulate or damage locking systems during an assessment.

## 6. Surveillance and monitoring

Evaluate whether security events can be detected, recorded and investigated.

Review:

- CCTV coverage.
- Camera placement.
- Image quality.
- Recording retention.
- Time synchronisation.
- Monitoring responsibilities.
- Alarm notification.
- Intrusion detection.
- Security patrol procedures.
- Incident escalation.

Where possible, validate monitoring using approved test events rather than creating real security incidents.

## 7. Restricted infrastructure

Assess physical protection around sensitive technology and infrastructure.

Examples:

- Server rooms.
- Network distribution rooms.
- Data centres.
- Telecommunications facilities.
- Backup storage.
- Power distribution.
- Building management systems.
- Network cabling infrastructure.

Review whether access is restricted to authorised personnel and whether entry is logged.

## 8. Controlled validation

Validate suspected weaknesses using approved, non-destructive techniques.

A finding should establish:

1. The security control being assessed.
2. The expected behaviour.
3. The observed behaviour.
4. The resulting security exposure.
5. The potential business impact.
6. Supporting evidence.

Do not exceed the agreed test boundaries to prove a finding.

## 9. Evidence collection

Capture evidence that supports each observation.

Examples:

- Photographs of approved areas.
- Screenshots of access-control configuration.
- Sanitised access logs.
- CCTV coverage diagrams.
- Interview notes.
- Test records.
- Policy documentation.
- Maintenance records.

Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information.

## 10. Risk assessment

Assess findings based on:

- Likelihood of exploitation.
- Potential impact.
- Asset sensitivity.
- Existing compensating controls.
- Ease of detection.
- Business consequences.
- Safety implications.

Distinguish between a confirmed weakness and a theoretical concern.

## 11. Reporting

A professional report should include:

- Executive summary.
- Assessment scope.
- Methodology.
- Testing limitations.
- Findings and severity.
- Supporting evidence.
- Business impact.
- Recommended remediation.
- Positive observations.
- Retest requirements.

## 12. Remediation and retesting

Work with the organisation to validate remediation.

Confirm that:

- Identified access weaknesses are corrected.
- Credentials and permissions are reviewed.
- Monitoring coverage is improved.
- Security procedures are updated.
- Responsible personnel understand the changes.
- Evidence of remediation is retained.
