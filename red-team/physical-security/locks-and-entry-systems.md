# Locks and Entry Systems

A reference for assessing mechanical and electronic locking systems used to protect buildings, rooms, cabinets and restricted infrastructure.

The assessment focuses on suitability, maintenance, administration and security management rather than destructive entry techniques.

## Assessment objectives

- Identify locking systems in use.
- Evaluate their suitability for the protected asset.
- Review key and credential management.
- Assess electronic locking controls.
- Review emergency access arrangements.
- Identify maintenance and configuration weaknesses.
- Evaluate the management of master keys and shared access.

## 1. Mechanical locking systems

Common mechanical systems include:

- Pin tumbler locks.
- Wafer locks.
- Lever locks.
- Mortice locks.
- Deadbolts.
- Padlocks.
- Cabinet locks.
- Restricted key systems.

Assess:

- Lock condition.
- Suitability for the application.
- Installation quality.
- Maintenance records.
- Key management arrangements.
- Environmental exposure.
- Compatibility with the door or enclosure.

Do not attempt to pick, bypass, force or damage locks during an assessment unless a specific controlled test has been separately authorised and safely arranged.

## 2. Electronic locking systems

Electronic systems may include:

- Electromagnetic locks.
- Electric strikes.
- Motorised locks.
- Electronic cabinet locks.
- Smart locks.
- Network-connected access-control locks.

Review:

- Authentication requirements.
- Administrative access.
- Credential management.
- Power failure behaviour.
- Emergency release.
- System logging.
- Maintenance arrangements.
- Vendor support.
- Network connectivity.

## 3. Key management

Physical keys remain a significant security consideration.

Review:

- Key inventories.
- Key assignment records.
- Key return procedures.
- Lost key reporting.
- Key duplication controls.
- Master-key management.
- Spare key storage.
- Contractor key access.
- Periodic key audits.

A key that cannot be accounted for may represent an unresolved security exposure.

## 4. Master-key systems

Master-key arrangements can simplify facility management but increase the consequences of key compromise.

Assess:

- Who holds master keys?
- Who approves their use?
- Where are they stored?
- Are key holders documented?
- Are master-key changes recorded?
- Are compromised keys replaced?
- Are restricted keys appropriately controlled?

## 5. Electronic access and lock integration

Review the relationship between locking hardware and access-control software.

Consider:

- Reader authentication.
- Controller configuration.
- Lock activation.
- Door-position monitoring.
- Access event logging.
- Power and network dependencies.
- Firmware maintenance.
- Emergency release mechanisms.

Confirm that system behaviour matches documented security requirements.

## 6. Fail-safe and fail-secure behaviour

Understand how locking systems behave during faults or emergencies.

- **Fail-safe:** The locking mechanism releases under a defined failure condition.
- **Fail-secure:** The locking mechanism remains secured under a defined failure condition.

The correct configuration depends on the application, building design and applicable safety requirements.

Never recommend a change without considering fire safety, emergency egress and relevant building regulations.

## 7. Emergency access

Review:

- Emergency release arrangements.
- Fire system integration.
- Emergency override procedures.
- Keyholder responsibilities.
- Emergency responder access.
- Documented testing.
- Maintenance and inspection records.

Emergency access must remain functional and compliant with life-safety requirements.

## 8. Physical condition and maintenance

Inspect approved systems for:

- Visible damage.
- Corrosion.
- Loose hardware.
- Fault indicators.
- Poor installation.
- Inconsistent operation.
- Missing maintenance records.
- Outdated components.

Report defects without attempting unauthorised repairs.

## 9. Controlled validation

Possible approved validation activities include:

- Reviewing lock specifications.
- Inspecting installation records.
- Testing designated electronic credentials.
- Reviewing access-control logs.
- Confirming emergency release operation through approved procedures.
- Reviewing key inventories.
- Verifying maintenance records.

Do not interfere with operational locks or emergency systems.

## Common findings

| Finding | Potential impact |
|---|---|
| Uncontrolled key duplication | Reduced control over physical access |
| Unaccounted master keys | Increased exposure across multiple areas |
| Poorly maintained locking hardware | Reduced reliability |
| Unreviewed electronic access | Unnecessary access permissions |
| Inadequate emergency arrangements | Safety and operational risks |
| Unsupported electronic lock firmware | Increased technical exposure |

## Remediation

- Maintain accurate key inventories.
- Restrict master-key access.
- Review key duplication arrangements.
- Establish maintenance schedules.
- Replace damaged hardware.
- Review electronic lock configurations.
- Maintain emergency access procedures.
- Validate safety and compliance requirements.
